# The look

Two things hold the look together across a whole video.

- **The style brief** is a paragraph of text describing how the video is drawn.
- **The style sample** is one image made from that brief.

## Starting points

Somewhere to start, not a closed set. Pick one, blend two, or describe something else entirely if the
brand calls for it.

- **Flat vector** — forms carried by shape rather than outline, and any outline that exists holds one
  weight throughout. No shading at all, or a single flat darker tone, never a gradient. A small
  saturated palette of three or four colours. Backgrounds are plain fields or simple geometry. Motion
  slides and scales rather than deforming. *SaaS, fintech, app demos, anything explaining how a thing
  works.*
- **2D cel / hand-drawn** — every form closed by an inked outline whose weight varies along it. Tone
  steps between two or three flat blocks with a hard shadow edge. Painted or flat backgrounds sitting
  behind the characters. Motion snaps between held poses, with squash and stretch on anything soft.
  *Character-led brand stories.*
- **Anime** — crisp linework of even weight, cel shading with a hard shadow edge and a bright rim along
  the top of forms, atmospheric painted backgrounds carrying less line detail than the figures, a deep
  palette with a few warm accents. Held poses with sharp transitions. *Youth, gaming, beauty, food.*
- **Stylized 3D character** — modelled rather than drawn, but simplified: heads and hands scaled up
  against the body, eyes big enough to carry the acting, corners rounded off everywhere. Surfaces carry
  only enough weave or grain to say what they're made of. One broad warm source, open fill, no hard
  shadow edge. Sets built and dressed, simplified to sit with the characters. Motion has weight — things
  overshoot and settle. *Mascots, kids, FMCG, toys, and what an ask naming a big animation studio is
  reaching for.*
- **Claymation / stop-motion** — everything reads as a physical object: fingerprint texture, slightly
  uneven surfaces, matte finish, visible seams. Real light with soft shadows on a built set. Motion
  steps rather than flows, with a faint judder. *Food, craft, sustainability, heritage.*
- **Paper cutout / collage** — flat layered shapes with torn or cut edges and visible paper grain,
  halftone or printed texture, bold flat colour fields. Backgrounds are assembled rather than painted.
  Motion slides and hinges as though the pieces were moved by hand. *Editorial, data stories,
  comparisons.*
- **Mono-line / minimal** — one line weight throughout, no fill or a single flat tone, enormous negative
  space, two or three colours at most. Backgrounds are close to empty. Motion is spare and precise, one
  thing moving at a time. *Premium, tech, medical, B2B.*
- **Isometric** — a fixed angled view with no perspective convergence, clean geometry, flat or lightly
  stepped shading picking out each face, a limited palette per system. Motion assembles, reveals and
  travels along the grid. *Logistics, infrastructure, systems, dashboards.*

**Where the user names a show or a film**, describe what that look is physically made of, using the
eight elements below. Naming the production alongside the description is fine. If the name trips a
refusal, that name is the likely cause and the description alone will usually pass — say so and let the
user decide whether to spend another call on it.

## The style brief

A style name on its own — *cartoon*, *anime*, *illustrated* — gets you the safest, most average version
of itself. What makes a look specific is **how the drawing is physically made**. Cover these eight
elements, a clause or two each.

1. **Line** — whether forms are outlined at all, how heavy the outline is, whether its weight varies
   along a form or holds even, and whether it reads as inked, pencilled, brushed or cut.
2. **Shape and proportion** — how far forms are simplified from life, and how a body is built: head
   against body, hands, eyes, how much is rounded off.
3. **Colour** — how saturated, whether it runs warm or cool, and the two or three colours a viewer would
   remember it by.
4. **Value** — how light is handled: stepped into flat blocks or blended continuously, how many steps,
   and whether the edge between light and shadow is hard or soft.
5. **Texture** — the surface quality of the drawing itself: paper tooth, canvas weave, grain, brush
   marks left standing, a printed halftone, or nothing at all.
6. **Level of detail** — how much is actually drawn and how much is implied.
7. **Backgrounds** — their treatment against the subject: painted, flat blocks, sparse or dense, and
   whether they sit at the same finish as the characters or deliberately behind them.
8. **Motion** — how it moves between poses: snapping between held drawings or moving smoothly, and how
   much a form squashes and stretches.

**Under a hundred words.** Long enough to cover all eight, short enough that none of them crowds out
the rest.

## What pulls a drawn look back toward a photograph

A drawn look only fails in one direction, and it doesn't take much. Steer clear of words like these:

- **Anything a lens does** — depth of field, focus falloff, flare, distortion, aberration.
- **Anything a film stock or a camera body is** — stock names, film grain, gear of any kind.
- **Anything a 3D renderer does with light** — traced light, scattering through a surface, contact
  shadows, volumetric beams.
- **The word *realistic*** in any form. On its own it undoes the rest of the paragraph.

**Two of the starting points are exceptions to the third one.** Claymation and stylized 3D are
*dimensional* looks — they are modelled, they are lit, they cast real shadows, and claymation is a built
set photographed under lamps. Words like *soft shadow*, *built set*, *matte surface* and *physical
texture* belong in those briefs; they are what the look is, not drift toward a photograph. What still
doesn't belong in any look is the camera: no lens behaviour, no film stock, no *realistic*.

## The style sample

One image, drawn from the style brief before any asset exists, then attached as a reference to every
asset call. Delivery ratio.

**It has no subject.** Nothing recognisable belongs in it — not a figure, not a place, not an object,
not a word. It is an abstract arrangement of the marks the style is made of: how the line behaves, how a
fill behaves, what happens at the edge of a shape, and the colours those come out in.

What that means varies by look. A cut-paper style shows its ragged edges, its printed dot texture and
its layered blocks of colour. A cel style shows a contour of varying weight beside the stepped tones it
would close around. A mono-line style shows one unvarying stroke crossing itself in open space.

Nothing from this video goes in it. A character, a place or a product drawn into the sample comes back
in every asset drawn against it.

**No colour chips.** No swatch bars, no palette strips, no grid of colour squares. The model reads
stripes as part of the style and paints them into the finished frames. Colour belongs in the sample as
the marks' own colour, never as a chart of it.
