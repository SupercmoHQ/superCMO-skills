# Recomposing an ad for a new shape

Changing an ad's proportions creates area that did not exist before, or takes area away. What goes into
that space, and what moves to make room, is the whole craft of a resize.

## Treat the ad as parts, not as a picture

Before anything moves, separate what the ad is made of: the hero product, the background it sits in,
each block of copy, the logo, and any decorative graphic. A resize rearranges those parts and re-spaces
them. It does not scale the picture.

That distinction is the difference between an ad that reads as designed for the placement and one that
reads as something squeezed into it.

## What grows is the background, not everything

The product keeps its presence in the frame — roughly the same visual weight it had, at the same
believable scale. The **background is what extends** into the new area, carried on in keeping with the
light direction, perspective and materials already there.

Scaling every element up together is the commonest way a resize goes wrong: the product bloats, the copy
turns coarse, and the ad loses the composure it had.

## Never do these

Each of them tells the viewer the ad was made for somewhere else:

- **Letterboxing or pillarboxing** — bars, blurred fills or a duplicated blurred copy of the image
  behind itself. It surrenders a large share of premium mobile screen and reads as repurposed.
- **Centre-cropping** to the new ratio — it discards the compositional intent and cuts elements in half.
- **Stretching or squashing** to fit.
- **Scaling the whole layout uniformly** rather than re-spacing it.

## What fills the new area

In order of preference:

1. **More of the environment the ad already has** — the surface, the backdrop, the scene, extended
   consistently. This is the default answer and usually the right one.
2. **Negative space around the product** — deliberate breathing room. It concentrates attention on the
   product, and it gives the platform's own caption and buttons somewhere to sit that isn't on top of
   the ad.
3. **Redistribution of what is already there** — copy that was crowded beside the product can take its
   own band; a logo cramped into a corner can sit properly.

**Never invent new elements to fill space.** A badge, a claim, a statistic, a second product, a stock
model that wasn't in the original — none of these are the skill's to add, and a fabricated claim in an
ad is worse than an empty corner. Where the space genuinely cannot be filled from what the ad has, say
so rather than manufacturing something.

## Going taller — a square or wide ad into `9:16`

The eye travels top to bottom, so the arrangement follows it: the hook or headline high in the frame,
the product through the middle where it holds attention, the offer or call to action below it — but
still above the band the platform covers.

Most of the height that appears is **background and breathing room, not more content**. A vertical ad
that tries to fill its extra space with extra words reads as cluttered, and the words land in the
covered band anyway.

## Going wider — a vertical or square ad into `16:9` or `1:1`

Space appears at the sides instead. The eye runs left to right, so the usual arrangement puts the
product to one side and lets the copy sit in the negative space opposite it. A wide frame carries less
copy than a tall one, not more — there is no room to build an argument, so the headline does the work.

## Check the result the way a designer would

- **The squint test** — blur your eyes; does the main message still arrive first?
- **The thumbnail test** — at feed size, are the headline and the call to action still legible?
- **The three-second test** — after a glance, is it clear what the ad is for?

## Start from the best source available

The higher the resolution of the ad handed over, the more real detail there is to rebuild from. Where
the user has a larger original than the one they supplied, it is worth asking for it before generating.
