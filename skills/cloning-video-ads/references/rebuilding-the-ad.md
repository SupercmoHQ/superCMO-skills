# Rebuilding the ad

A clone keeps what makes the reference ad work and swaps in the user's product. The craft is separating the two: the **form** is kept exactly — the shots, the timing, the camera, the pacing, the sound; the **identity** is replaced — the product, the brand and the spoken words become the user's. Get the split right and the clone feels like the same ad made for a different product, not a different ad. Work from the timed breakdown of the reference (`references/reading-the-ad.md`).

## What to keep

The blueprint holds the reference ad's structure exactly: the same shots in the same order, each the same length, with the same camera work, the same pacing, the same light and colour, the same on-screen-text style, the same audio register, and the same arc — the hook, the turn, and the close all landing where they land in the original. This is the whole value of a clone; don't improve it or reorder it.

## What to swap

Only the identity changes:

- **The product** — the user's, in place of the original's, in the same spot in every shot the original's product held.
- **The spoken words** — rewritten for the user's product (see below).
- **The brand** — the original brand's name, logo, wordmark, slogans and any on-screen marks are gone. The only brand in the clone is the user's, and only where the reference showed one.
- **The hand-interaction** — where the original did something to its product (a tap, a twist, a pour), re-fit the move to how the user's product actually works, keeping the same framing and beat. A cap-off becomes whatever the user's product opens with; a screen-tap becomes whatever it does.
- **The people** — where a person appears, cast them to suit the user's product and its buyer, not whoever was in the original. Keep their role and framing; change who they are.

## Holding the product true

The product on screen is the exact one in the reference image(s), reaching every render unchanged:

- **Same everything the image(s) show** — label, colours, shape, proportions, finish. Only the sides the reference image(s) show are ever visible; no back, interior or unseen face the images don't carry ever appears, even in a shot that orbits it.
- **Never fabricate or embellish it.** Don't ask the model to redraw the printed label — rendered text and logos warp; the label rides in from the reference image(s) as it is. Don't invent a variant or add a feature that isn't in the image(s).
- **The same object throughout.** The product image(s) are passed into the clip as references and described in the same words each time. Written descriptions alone drift; the reference is what holds it.
- **True size.** The product stays the size it really is against a hand or a surface. Where fine print won't resolve, tighten the framing on it rather than blowing the product up to force the text legible.

## The spoken lines

Rewrite what is said for the user's product, matching the original line for line: the same number of beats, each about the same length, in the same place in the ad, with the same energy. Keep the cadence and swap the content — the original's claims and brand name go, the user's claims (and only the user's) take their place. Where the original made a claim the user hasn't supplied, don't carry it over and don't invent one; find a line that fits the beat from what the user did give. The words are heard as a voiceover over the picture, never lip-synced on camera.

## On-screen text

Text is where an ad most often breaks — the model warps lettering, and small type comes back as gibberish. So the clone keeps only the text the reference genuinely needs:

- **Text on the product is fine.** Text the product carries on its own surface — etched, embossed, printed — reads cleanly, because the model renders it as part of the material. Keep to what the product actually shows.
- **Overlay text only where the reference ad has it**, and even then sparingly — large, and only at the open or the close, never over the shots between. Small or coloured type warps.
- The only brand shown is the user's.

## The sound

The clip model writes sound with the picture in one pass — a general cue, not a mix conducted shot to shot.

- **Declare the sound — silence is not the default.** A prompt that says nothing about sound still comes back scored, usually like a generic advert. Name the ambience and the few sounds the action makes, matching the reference ad's register; where the ad should be quiet, say so plainly.
- **Effects and ambient are the safe, strong layer.** Name the source and the surface a sound happens on, keep to two or three at once, and tie a key sound to its moment. These are generic and copyright-clean.
- **Music: give a mood, a genre, a tempo — never a named song or artist**, and never the reference ad's actual track. The model composes its own from that. A named or recognisable piece risks the model's audio copyright filter.
- **Spoken lines** — any spoken line is a voiceover over the picture, never lip-synced on camera.

## What a clone never does

A clone copies *craft*, not content. It never reproduces the reference ad's own footage, its brand name, logo, wordmark or slogan, or the likeness of the people in it. What carries across is the structure and the technique — the shot order, the timing, the camera, the pacing — rebuilt fresh with the user's product and brand. If a shot can't be described without naming or showing the original brand, it has been copied too closely; describe what the shot *does* and let the user's product fill it.
