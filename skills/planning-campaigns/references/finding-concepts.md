# Finding the concepts

This file turns the gathered context into **campaigns, each holding the concepts worth testing in it**. A campaign is one distinct go-to-market push. A concept is one ad, described as an argument: who it is for, what it claims, and how the ad makes that land.

**A campaign is a set of different arguments, not one argument reworded.** Ads that look and read alike are treated by the ad platform as variations of one creative, not as different ads.

## Step 1 — Read what the data says

Every claim a concept makes traces to something below. No spend, reach, click, cost or conversion figure exists for any ad in the research; run length is the only performance proxy.

**From the audit of our own ads:**

- **Held** — arguments with long runs and variants beside them. Proven with this audience.
- **Failed** — arguments stopped fast. Tests that failed.
- **Never tried** — the axes with no ads on them: arguments, offers, casts, audiences.

**From the competitor analysis:**

- **Validated** — patterns shared by the long-runners of more than one competitor.
- **Worn out** — the claims every competitor makes.
- **Absent** — arguments nobody in the category runs; and arguments present among the stopped ads but gone from the live ones, which were tried and dropped.

**From the brand and the product:**

- The proof points, quoted as recorded. Anything marked unconfirmed is not evidence.
- What the product does and what problems it solves.

**From the customer's words** — reviews or comments the brief carries, and ads in the research where a customer speaks. **Keep the phrasing, not a paraphrase of it:** the pain in their words, what triggered the search, what they doubted before buying, what changed after. This is the strongest material in the file; where there is none, say so, because every concept is then written in the brand's guess at its buyer's language rather than the buyer's own.

## Step 2 — Decide which campaigns to run

Split concepts into separate campaigns only where the findings show one of these:

- **A market or a language** the brand sells into separately from the rest.
- **A product line or vertical** with its own buyers and its own proof.
- **A season, a launch or an event** with a start and an end, kept apart so its swings don't drag on everything running year-round.

**Nothing else splits a campaign unless the user explicitly asks for it.** Not the kind of customer being addressed, and not the argument being made — the ad platform reads the creative to find its audience, so splitting by customer type narrows delivery instead of widening it, and different arguments are the point of a campaign rather than a reason to start another one. Where none of the three applies, everything is one campaign, and that is the ordinary answer rather than a thin one.

**Give each campaign a name, and a line or two saying what it sells and to whom, and why it is worth running now** — within the ceiling the workflow settled. Say which of the three above separates it from the rest, and what evidence shows that separation is real.

## Step 3 — Draft the candidates

**Draft candidates per campaign up to the count the workflow settled, working down the findings in this order:**

1. **What held.** Rebuild a proven argument a different way: a sharper version of the same claim, or the same claim delivered differently. Label it a refresh.
2. **What the category proves.** Where more than one competitor's long-runners share a pattern, draft this brand's version of it — this product's proof, this brand's voice, never the competitor ads' words, footage or structure. A pattern holding for one competitor alone is a thinner signal; the candidate says so.
3. **What nobody runs, and what we have never tried.** The strongest position in the plan and the least evidenced one, so the candidate carries the reason the gap is an opening rather than a dead end — what in the product or the proof lets this brand make an argument the category doesn't.
4. **What failed or was dropped.** A candidate only where something is different this time, and it states what.
5. **The product and the brand alone.** For what the research doesn't reach. Label these reasoned; everything above is evidenced.

Each candidate is a description of the ad plus four fields. The concept and its four fields are the whole handoff — the producer that builds it reads this and nothing else from the plan — so **write it in full** : what the ad opens on, who is in it, what happens beat by beat, what is said or shown, and how it resolves. Write what is filmed or framed, the person, the action, the thing shown, never adjectives about the kind of ad it is: "engaging", "authentic" and "relatable" describe no ad and leave the producer to invent what it actually is.

Each field carries enough for a fresh reader to build the ad faithfully:

- **The buyer** — who it is for, as the job they are trying to get done and what made them start looking, and how much they already know. 
- **The hypothesis** — the one thing this concept is betting on, stated so it could be wrong: that this buyer will respond to this argument in this form. Say what a win would prove that the account doesn't already know — a new buyer worth chasing, an argument worth scaling, a format that travels — so a result changes what gets made next rather than just winning or losing on its own.
- **The evidence** — everything the concept rests on, laid out so the producer needn't re-research it: the finding and its counts, or the plain observation about the product, the brand or the buyer; the proof points the ad may use, quoted from the brand profile. Where it draws on another ad, give the local downloaded copy — preferred — or its ad-library URL, and say what to take from it and what to leave, never a reference code that resolves to nothing. 
- **The format** — what the ad is: a video and roughly how long, a single image ad, a carousel and what its cards do or something else.

Three rules bind every candidate:

- **No invented proof.** Every statistic, rating, testimonial, award or result comes from the brand profile or the user, quoted as recorded.
- **No invented commercial terms.** An offer, price, discount, trial or CTA exists only where the brand profile or the user states it. 
- **No claim the product cannot support.** The product facts bound what can be argued, and anything the brand profile marked unconfirmed never becomes a hook, a headline or a button.

## Step 4 — Select what each campaign keeps

Score each candidate on **impact** — how much it would change for the account if it won — and **confidence** — how strong the evidence behind it is. How easy it is to build is not a criterion. Keep the highest-scoring candidates in each campaign, down to the count the workflow settled.

Then check the survivors, and fix what fails:

- Two concepts are distinct only if they differ in more than the wording — a different buyer, a different bet, a different ad.
- **Nothing the account already runs**, unless it is labelled a refresh.
- **Cut, never merge.** Don't pad a thin set back up with rewordings of a survivor. Stopping short because the evidence ran out is a finding — say so rather than filling the count.

## Step 5 — Write the concept list

Write the campaigns and their concepts to `concepts.md` in the run's folder. One section per campaign in the order settled, and within each, its concepts in score order.

| Per campaign | What goes in it |
| --- | --- |
| Heading | The campaign's name, and the line saying what it sells, to whom, why now, and what separates it from the others. |
| Concepts | Each concept in score order — its full description, then its buyer, its hypothesis, its evidence and its format, and whether it is evidenced or reasoned. |

Close the file with what was left out and why — the arguments the findings hinted at but the evidence couldn't carry, and any campaign that stopped short because the evidence ran out.
